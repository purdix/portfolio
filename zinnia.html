The Problem

A system that wasn't 'system-ing'

When I first joined Rose Rocket, the design system wasn’t really a system at all. The Figma file was disorganized, components were scattered and inconsistent, and the product's UI was a Frankenstein of various out-of-the-box libraries. This created a number of painful challenges for our team:

🧩 No single source of truth

Designers and developers worked in silos without a shared system, leading to misalignment and duplicated effort.

🔧 Reinvented patterns

Lack of reusable components meant devs had to hardcode UI repeatedly—wasting time and introducing avoidable inconsistencies.

🐢 Slow R&D velocity

Without a unified design system, building even simple interfaces took longer and required constant clarification and rework.

🎨 Inconsistent UI & UX

Core patterns, such as buttons, forms, and tables, looked and behaved differently across the app, creating a fragmented experience.

The Solution

Rebuilding from the ground up

To fix the chaos, I took the lead in overhauling our design system. This wasn’t just a cleanup. It was about creating a scalable foundation that brought alignment, consistency, and speed across teams.

Step 1: Audit & align

I kicked off the rebuild by conducting a full audit of our design system. I reviewed every component, catalogued inconsistencies and identified accessibility issues. This became the foundation for prioritization and helped align the team on where to focus our efforts.

A screenshot of the audit spreadsheet showing notes, links, pass/fail scoring, and priorities.

Step 2: Revamp accessibility

One of the biggest gaps I uncovered was our existing colour scale. It was manually created, with uneven steps and poor accessibility.

Using Leonardo, I rebuilt the palette from the ground up—systematizing colours by contrast and expanding the scale from 50 to 900. Now, every midtone (500+) meets WCAG 2.0 on white backgrounds, giving us a solid foundation for accessible design.

Image depicting the updated color scale built using contrast-based tooling.

We also introduced new hues to better support future needs and improve design flexibility.

Before-and-after comparison of our colour palette.

Step 3: Rebuild & tokenize

With the foundation set, our team began rebuilding every component from the ground up. Many of them were outdated, using frames, groups, and detached styles, which made them hard to maintain. We updated them using modern Figma features like Auto Layout and Variant properties to make them more scalable and consistent.

Screenshot of our refactored field input, showcasing boolean, text, and other variant properties.

In parallel, I led the effort to introduce design tokens. I defined the naming conventions, structure, and architecture from scratch, creating a bridge between Figma and code. To support adoption, I hosted a couple company-wide sessions explaining how tokens worked, why they mattered and how to use them effectively.

Left: FigJam brainstorm of the token structure. Right: Finalized tokens & modes implemented in Figma.

Screenshots from the two company-wide presentations.

Step 4: Organize and scale the system

To bring structure to the chaos, I introduced an Atoms & Molecules framework to standardize how we organized components. This modular system helped designers and devs quickly find and reuse the building blocks they needed without recreating UI from scratch.

Screenshots from the two company-wide presentations.

Step 5: Build for speed with UI kits

To help the team move faster, I created UI Kits—pre-built layouts for our most commonly used flows:

  • Board layout: for data-dense table views
  • Records layout: for detailed object views (side panel, modal, full screen)
  • Widgets: for smaller UI blocks representing key actions

These templates turned repeated UI into plug-and-play patterns, reducing design time and ensuring consistency.
